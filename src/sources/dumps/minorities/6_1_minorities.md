6.1 NLP Modelling of minority Related Science

Research Question: Beyond the three DigiCHer pilot communities, which minority groups in Europe should HM support as filterable, self-identified categories, and how can this list be derived transparently rather than through an externally imposed taxonomy?

Objective: Produce a documented, reproducible list of European minority groups that can be enriched with the term categories (self-designation, language, spatial reference) established for the pilot communities in section 3.1, and exposed as a minority filter dimension in the platform. This directly extends Priority 2 (Chapter 11): expanding minority coverage from the three pilot groups to a broader, EU-wide set.

Approach

Following the reasoning in section 3.1 (Minorities Revisited), the project avoids externally assigning minority status wherever possible and instead builds on Wikidata as an open, collaboratively maintained knowledge infrastructure. Rather than starting from a fixed, externally curated list of "official" minorities, the pipeline over-generates a broad candidate pool of ethnic, tribal, and indigenous groups associated with European countries, reduces this pool through a small number of documented, reproducible filtering criteria, and then treats the resulting list as a transparent, public starting point rather than a closed, expert-finalised taxonomy. This mirrors the citizen science and co-creation approach that defines DIGICHer more broadly: rather than the project deciding unilaterally which groups qualify as minorities, the list is published together with its derivation logic and the keywords used to detect each group, so that communities, institutions, and consortium partners can review, contest, and correct it after launch.

Candidate Harvesting

Candidates are discovered via SPARQL against the Wikidata Query Service, querying for entities classified (P31) as ethnic group, tribe, indigenous people, or ethnoreligious group, restricted to an explicit allowlist of European countries (EU27, EEA states, the UK, the Western Balkans, and EU-candidate Eastern Partnership countries), plus a second query anchored on "indigenous to" (P2341) for groups such as Sámi subgroups whose homeland (Sápmi) is a region rather than a sovereign state. Three pilot groups structurally invisible to this discovery process (Ladin, Sámi, Jewish) are injected as manual seeds, since their Wikidata entries lack the country properties the discovery query relies on. Obvious sub-population noise ("Albanians in Norway"-style entries naming a country in the label itself) is removed automatically before output.

Each surviving candidate is enriched with ten filter dimensions sourced directly from Wikidata properties: population (P1082), country (P17), religion (P140), native language (P103), parent grouping (P361), taxonomic subclass (P279), diaspora status (P3833), ancestral home (P66), administrative territory (P131), and constituent subgroups (P527). This harvest currently yields 451 candidate groups, stored in candidate_groups.csv alongside their QIDs for re-querying as Wikidata labels and classifications evolve.

Reduction to a Working List

451 candidates include entries that cannot meaningfully function as a minority filter option at all, independent of any judgement about minority status itself. Three mechanical, auditable criteria were applied to remove these:

Unresolved labels: Candidates without a resolvable English name (Wikidata returns only a QID, e.g. Q10262245) are excluded, since they cannot be surfaced in the UI or matched against project text without a name to search for. This removes 45 entries.

Diaspora typing: Candidates that Wikidata itself classifies as a diaspora (P3833) of another nation's population are excluded, e.g. "British Bangladeshi," "Germans of Hungary," "Indo people." These represent populations defined by recent, largely economic migration rather than long-term, territorially rooted communities, and are excluded on this basis rather than through a manually maintained blocklist, keeping the exclusion reproducible from Wikidata's own typing. This removes 35 entries.

Residual non-European association: A small number of candidates carry only non-European countries (Mongolia, Kazakhstan), an artifact of the "indigenous to" discovery query anchoring on continent rather than the strict country allowlist used elsewhere. These are excluded as out of scope for a platform focused on European CH. This removes 37 entries.

Applied together, these criteria reduce the candidate pool from 451 to 338 groups (accounting for 4 candidates flagged by more than one criterion). This is a mechanical, fully reproducible step: the criteria, not a person's judgement of which groups "count," determine the outcome, and are documented here precisely so they can be reviewed and challenged.

Design Decision: Broad Coverage Over Manual Pre-Selection

A further manual reduction toward a smaller, hand-picked shortlist was considered and rejected. Inspection of the remaining 338 groups shows that many of the harder cases do not resolve cleanly into "minority" or "not minority" at all. Groups such as Finns or Italians appear in the dataset tagged to several countries at once (Finns: Finland, Sweden, Estonia, Latvia, United Kingdom) because Wikidata attaches country associations directly to the ethnic group itself rather than distinguishing where that group is a demographic majority from where it is a recognised minority. Finns are the titular majority in Finland and simultaneously a legally recognised national minority in Sweden; the same entity represents both. A manual rule that drops or keeps such entries wholesale would either erase legitimate, policy-relevant national minorities or reintroduce exactly the kind of externally imposed judgement about identity that section 3.1 argues against.

Rather than resolving these cases internally before launch, the project treats the 338-group list as the working set exposed through the platform's minority filter, together with the pre-aggregated keyword sets used to detect each group in project and publication text. This decision follows directly from the transparency and explainability principle already established for minority detection in section 2.4: keyword-based matching was chosen over an opaque classifier precisely so that every match remains inspectable, and the same logic extends to the list of groups itself. A transparency page (planned per Priority 2, Chapter 11) will document, for each of the 338 groups, its source QID, the keywords used to identify it, and match statistics against the corpus, allowing consortium partners, minority communities, and the wider public to flag groups that are mis-scoped, contest a classification, or propose additions the SPARQL harvest missed. This positions the minority filter as a living, community-correctable resource rather than a fixed taxonomy finalised by the project team, consistent with DIGICHer's broader citizen science and co-creation methodology described in the Project Overview.

This approach also reflects a practical constraint: automated disambiguation of majority-versus-minority status per country, per group, is a research problem in its own right and is not solvable with the data currently modelled in core_v3. Publishing broad and transparent, with a clear feedback channel, is judged preferable to a narrower list whose exclusions could not be justified more rigorously than the ones already documented above.
